## A Water/Flying planning scenario

Imagine a team slot that can use either an Electric move or a Rock move. The typing-only answer is to test the Electric move first, because it pressures both parts of Cramorant’s type combination. If the actual move or format makes that option unavailable, Rock is the next clean chart-based check. A Ground move should not be selected merely because the attacker is strong in another matchup: Flying grants a Ground immunity in the standard chart.

## Original judgment: when Electric is correct and when it is not enough

The useful planning judgment is not simply “use Electric.” Electric is the correct first filter because it attacks both Water and Flying, but it should not be the last filter in Pokémon GO. A weak Electric fast move with poor energy pressure can still lose a practical race to a better-neutral option if Cramorant’s released moves force shields, bait poorly, or punish the Electric attacker’s own typing. In raids or Max-style group content, the Electric-first rule is stronger because shield timing is absent and raw super-effective pressure tends to matter more. In PvP, the rule is only a starting point: energy generation, move timing, shield advantage, and whether the Electric attacker can survive Cramorant’s charged moves matter just as much.

That creates three decision tiers. First, prefer an Electric attacker that already fits the format and does not require a special one-use investment. Second, compare Rock only when the Electric option is unavailable, underleveled, or has a clearly worse move profile. Third, avoid “famous strong Pokémon” picks that miss the typing question entirely. A Ground specialist, for example, may be excellent elsewhere, but Cramorant’s Flying half makes Ground the wrong baseline answer in the standard chart and an inefficient direction to investigate first in Pokémon GO.

For team building, Cramorant also asks you to separate countering from covering. An Electric answer pressures Cramorant, but it may invite Ground-type responses in formats where switching matters. A Rock answer may hit Cramorant but often brings Water or Grass vulnerability elsewhere. If you are preparing a cup team, pair the Cramorant answer with a teammate that handles the likely anti-Electric or anti-Rock responses. If you are preparing PvE, prioritize the highest powered Electric damage you can field without starving resources from more common Water/Flying, Flying, and Water targets.

## Form-change implications

Cramorant’s form-change note is the part that makes this article worth keeping instead of treating it as a disposable event page. A form change can alter battle texture even when it does not alter the Water/Flying type line. TypeMatchup’s calculator can tell you which attack types should be favored, but it cannot decide whether a form-change trigger changes move pacing, animation reads, bait risk, or the opponent’s incentive to shield. That distinction matters because players often overread type charts as complete battle forecasts.

If Cramorant’s form behavior is relevant in a live format, use a two-pass review. First, calculate the type baseline: Electric first, Rock second, avoid resisted lines. Second, watch the actual battle loop: does the form change happen often enough to affect shield decisions, or is it mostly flavor during the matchup? If the mechanic changes only presentation, build as you would against a Water/Flying Pokémon. If it changes pressure or timing, prefer steadier counters over fragile “perfect type” answers that collapse after one bad shield call.
